05Education

Incoming PhD — Artificial Intelligence Cognitive Distillation and Schema Formation for Autonomous AI Agents Supervisors: Prof. Okba Tibermacine Incoming

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled the development of autonomous AI agents capable of sophisticated reasoning, planning, tool use, web navigation, and interaction with external environments. Despite these capabilities, current agents lack structured and persistent mechanisms for learning from experience — most systems store interaction histories as raw textual traces and retrieve them when faced with similar situations, resulting in high computational costs, limited scalability, and reduced robustness.

This doctoral project bridges human cognitive development and AI by introducing the concept of cognitive distillation: rather than retaining every detail of an experience, agents will abstract recurring patterns into compact mental structures — or schemas — that guide future behavior. The research designs, implements, and formally analyzes a new class of autonomous agents capable of continuously transforming raw experiences into organized, executable knowledge schemas: a structured "mental playbook" enabling efficient decision-making, adaptation, and expertise acquisition. It investigates mechanisms for schema formation, consolidation, maintenance, and forgetting, aiming to establish a principled framework for autonomous expertise development in AI agents.

Research Problem. Current autonomous agents operating in environments such as web navigation, software interaction, and complex virtual worlds typically record every observation, action, and outcome as part of an interaction history, then retrieve and process large amounts of historical data when facing similar tasks. This approach gives rise to three major challenges:

  • Inefficiency: processing extensive interaction histories for every decision requires significant computational resources, increasing inference latency, memory consumption, and operational costs.
  • Fragility: agents rely on literal similarities between current and past experiences rather than on abstract behavioral knowledge, so even minor environmental changes can significantly degrade performance.
  • Information Overload: as interaction histories grow, memory repositories become saturated with redundant, noisy, and outdated information; existing systems lack principled mechanisms to distinguish valuable knowledge from irrelevant details.

These limitations highlight the need for memory architectures capable of transforming episodic experiences into compact, reusable, and adaptive knowledge structures.
